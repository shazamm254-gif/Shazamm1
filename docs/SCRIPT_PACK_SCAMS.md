# The Setup — Full Voiceover Script Pack (Top 10)

Complete, ready-to-record scripts for concepts **#1–10** of
[`docs/IDEA_BANK_SCAMS.md`](IDEA_BANK_SCAMS.md). Niche config:
`tools/niche-scams.json`.

Every case below is real and publicly documented. Where a script follows a
*pattern* rather than one named case, it says so — dramatized composite, never
invented detail, never a real private individual named.

**Delivery:** calm, low, forensic. Never gleeful. ~2.5 words per second, so a
120-word VO lands around 48 seconds. Hook inside 2 seconds, cut visuals every
1.5–3s, end on the loop line.

**Every script follows the 6-beat structure:** impossible fact → make it normal
→ one mechanism → the escalation → the tell → the turn. The "make it normal"
beat is the one people cut when they're rushing. It's the beat that holds
retention, because it's the beat that stops the video being mockery.

> **The line this channel does not cross.** These explain *how victims are
> manipulated* — never how to run the play. No tooling, no sourcing, no
> targeting, no step-by-step build. Every video ends on a tell the viewer can
> actually use. Nobody in the audience should ever finish one of these feeling
> superior to the victim; the thesis is always "this was built to work on you."

## 1 — "The wrong number" · *Pig butchering*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"'Sorry, wrong number.' That's the opening move of a billion-dollar scam."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** SORRY, WRONG NUMBER
- **VO:** "'Sorry, wrong number.' That's the opening move of a scam that takes billions a year. She apologises. You're polite back. Now you're talking. For the next four months she's just a friend — she never asks you for anything. Eventually she mentions the trading platform her uncle taught her to use. You look. You put in five hundred dollars. It grows. You withdraw it, and the money genuinely lands in your bank account. That withdrawal is the whole scam. It's a five-hundred-dollar receipt that buys your life savings, because next time you won't test it. Investigators call it pig butchering — you fatten the pig before slaughter. The fattening was the friendship."
- **VISUALS:** A single grey text bubble on a black phone screen → a thread scrolling upward for a long unbroken beat to show months of history → a trading dashboard with numbers ticking green → a bank notification: +$500, real, mundane → slow pull-back revealing the phone is one of forty on a long desk.
- **END:** *"It was never a wrong number. Yours was the only number they meant to text."*
- **TITLE:** The scam that starts with "sorry, wrong number"
- **DESC:** They let you win $500 so you'd never test it again. That's the entire mechanism. #scams #scamalert #psychology #fraud #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Pattern documented in FBI IC3 Internet Crime Reports and Global Anti-Scam Organisation casework; "sha zhu pan" / pig butchering. Composite, not one named victim.

## 2 — "Everyone on the call was fake" · *Deepfake fraud*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"He joined a video call with seven colleagues. Every one of them was a deepfake."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** ALL OF THEM
- **VO:** "A finance worker in Hong Kong received a message from his chief financial officer asking for a confidential transfer. It felt wrong. So he did the responsible thing — he asked for a video call. And there was the CFO, along with several colleagues he recognised. They discussed the transfer. He asked questions. They answered him. So he sent roughly twenty-five million dollars. Every person on that call was generated. He was the only human in the meeting. The engineering firm Arup later confirmed it publicly. Look closely at what actually happened here: his suspicion didn't fail. His suspicion worked perfectly — and then the fake simply answered it."
- **VISUALS:** A 3×3 video-call grid, faces soft and non-identifiable → one tile flickering into wireframe → a transfer confirmation screen → the grid again, now with every tile the same silhouette → one real chair in one real empty office.
- **END:** *"You don't beat this by looking harder. You beat it by hanging up and calling a number you already had."*
- **TITLE:** Everyone on the video call was a deepfake
- **DESC:** He asked for a video call to verify. That was the trap. ~$25M, Hong Kong, 2024. #deepfake #scams #ai #fraud #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Hong Kong Police, Jan–Feb 2024; confirmed by Arup, May 2024. ~HK$200M (≈US$25M).

## 3 — "The voice was hers" · *AI voice cloning*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"She heard her daughter crying down the phone. Her daughter was asleep upstairs."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** IT WASN'T HER
- **VO:** "She heard her daughter crying down the phone. Her daughter was asleep upstairs. A few seconds of audio — a birthday video, a voice note, anything public — is now enough to clone a voice well enough to fool a parent. But the voice isn't really the weapon. The weapon is the shape of the call: your child is hurt, there's a deadline, and don't hang up. Panic switches off the exact part of you that checks things. Parents who've had this call describe the same thing afterwards: they knew something was wrong and they could not make themselves stop. That's not stupidity. That's your nervous system doing precisely what it evolved to do."
- **VISUALS:** A phone face-up on a dark kitchen counter → an audio waveform slowly hollowing into something synthetic → a staircase with a light on at the top → a clock ticking → the same phone, screen now dark.
- **END:** *"Agree a family safe word tonight. It's free, and it's the only defence left when the voice is perfect."*
- **TITLE:** The AI voice scam that uses your own child
- **DESC:** Seconds of public audio is enough. Urgency is the real weapon. Pick a family safe word. #ai #voiceclone #scams #scamalert #shorts
- **SOURCE:** FTC consumer alerts on family-emergency and voice-cloning scams (2023–); pattern testified to the US Senate Judiciary Committee, June 2023. Composite.

## 4 — "The typos are on purpose" · *Why scam emails are badly written*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"That scam email is badly written on purpose. The spelling mistakes are a filter."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** ON PURPOSE
- **VO:** "That scam email is badly written on purpose. You've always assumed the typos meant a careless amateur. They mean the opposite. Sending a million emails costs almost nothing. What costs a fortune is the weeks a human spends talking to someone who was never going to pay. So the message is engineered to be unbelievable. Anyone with the slightest scepticism deletes it immediately — and that's the point, because they were never the target. A Microsoft researcher laid the maths out in 2012: an implausible story maximises profit by filtering out everyone who'd stop halfway. The absurdity isn't a bug in the scam. It's quality control."
- **VISUALS:** A scam email rendered huge in plain type → misspellings circling in red one at a time → a funnel: 1,000,000 emails narrowing to a handful of figures → the word FILTER stamped across the funnel.
- **END:** *"It wasn't stupid. It was screening for people who wouldn't stop."*
- **TITLE:** Scam emails are badly written on purpose
- **DESC:** The typos aren't incompetence — they're a filter that removes anyone sceptical enough to waste the scammer's time. #scams #psychology #phishing #fraud #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Cormac Herley, Microsoft Research, *Why Do Nigerian Scammers Say They Are From Nigeria?* (WEIS 2012).

## 5 — "Two cherries and a bar" · *The near-miss*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"You didn't nearly win. The machine decided to show you that."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** ALMOST
- **VO:** "Two cherries, and the third reel stops one symbol short. You didn't nearly win — you lost, exactly as completely as any other loss. But your brain doesn't file it that way. Near misses light up reward circuitry in a way that plain losses don't, and they make people play longer. Researchers have measured this since the eighties. Then there's the other one: you bet three dollars, you win two, and the machine erupts — lights, coins, fanfare. The industry has a term for that. Losses disguised as wins. You are down a dollar. Your body has just been congratulated. Do that a few hundred times an hour and the machine has trained you."
- **VISUALS:** Extreme macro on reels, the third stopping a hair too far → a brain diagram lighting identically for "almost" and "won" → a payout readout: BET $3 / WIN $2 with celebration graphics blazing → a wide, silent room of machines.
- **END:** *"You lost money, and your brain filed it as a victory."*
- **TITLE:** Slot machines are designed to *almost* pay
- **DESC:** Near misses and "losses disguised as wins" — the two mechanics that make you play longer. #casino #psychology #gambling #design #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Reid, *Journal of Gambling Behavior* (1986) on the near-miss; Dixon, Harrigan et al. on losses disguised as wins (2010–).

## 6 — "The coin with no blockchain" · *OneCoin*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"Billions of dollars went into a cryptocurrency that had no blockchain. None."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** NO BLOCKCHAIN
- **VO:** "Billions went into a cryptocurrency that had no blockchain. Prosecutors said the coins were simply numbers in a private database — a spreadsheet with a price its own owners typed in. And it sold out arenas. Because OneCoin wasn't sold as software, it was sold as membership. You didn't buy a coin, you bought educational packages, and you earned by recruiting. Every question got the same answer: you're early, the sceptics don't understand, the exchange opens soon. The exchange never opened. Its founder vanished in 2017 and is still on the FBI's most wanted list. The strangest part isn't that nobody checked the blockchain. It's that checking it would have taken one afternoon."
- **VISUALS:** A gleaming gold coin logo dissolving into an empty spreadsheet grid → silhouetted arena crowds under stage light → a blockchain diagram where every block is blank → an empty stage, one spotlight, no one on it.
- **END:** *"Nobody audited the coin, because everyone was too busy recruiting the next buyer."*
- **TITLE:** The $4 billion crypto with no blockchain
- **DESC:** Prosecutors said the "coins" were entries in a private database. It sold out arenas anyway. #crypto #scams #fraud #onecoin #shorts
- **SOURCE:** US DOJ / SDNY indictments and 2019 guilty plea of Konstantin Ignatov; Ruja Ignatova added to FBI Ten Most Wanted, June 2022.

## 7 — "Sold the Eiffel Tower" · *Victor Lustig, 1925*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"He sold the Eiffel Tower for scrap. Then he came back and sold it again."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** TWICE
- **VO:** "In 1925 a con man read that the Eiffel Tower was rusting and expensive to maintain. So he had government stationery printed, invited six scrap metal dealers to a hotel suite, and explained — confidentially — that the city had decided to demolish it. He picked the buyer who seemed least secure among them. That man paid for seven thousand tonnes of iron, plus a bribe to secure the contract, which is the detail that mattered: a bribe makes the buyer a participant. He was too humiliated to go to the police. And that silence was so reliable that Lustig came back to Paris months later and ran it a second time."
- **VISUALS:** 1920s Paris in fog, the tower as a cold grey cut-out → embossed government letterhead, macro → a hotel suite, six hats on a stand, no faces → a handshake seen only as two hands → an empty police desk.
- **END:** *"He didn't build a scam nobody would notice. He built one nobody could admit to."*
- **TITLE:** The man who sold the Eiffel Tower — twice
- **DESC:** He made the buyer complicit, so the buyer stayed silent. That's why he could run it again. #history #conartist #scams #truecrime #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Widely documented account of Victor Lustig and buyer André Poisson, 1925. Note: much of Lustig's biography rests on later journalistic retellings.

## 8 — "He invented a country" · *Poyais, 1820s*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"Two hundred settlers sailed to a country that did not exist."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** POYAIS
- **VO:** "In the 1820s, London investors bought government bonds for the nation of Poyais. There was a printed guidebook describing its fertile soil and its capital city. There was a constitution. There was currency. Emigrants sold their homes, converted their savings into Poyaisian dollars, and sailed. What they found was undeveloped coast. No capital, no port, nothing. Many of them died there. And here's the mechanism: nobody could travel eight thousand kilometres to check, so the documents themselves became the evidence. A map, a banknote and a printed book vouched for each other in a closed loop. He didn't forge a single document. He forged the reality the documents described."
- **VISUALS:** An ornate hand-tinted map with a coastline outlined in gold → an invented banknote, macro → a guidebook page describing a capital city → the same coastline as empty grey shoreline and jungle → a ship's rail, nobody at it.
- **END:** *"Everything they checked was real. It just all pointed at the same lie."*
- **TITLE:** He invented an entire country and sold it
- **DESC:** Bonds, currency, a constitution, a guidebook — for a nation that never existed. Settlers sailed anyway. #history #scams #conartist #truecrime #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Gregor MacGregor and the Poyais scheme, 1820s; guidebook published under the pseudonym Thomas Strangeways. Two settler ships sailed; most emigrants died or were evacuated.

## 9 — "The call that warns you" · *Bank impersonation*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"The best scam call doesn't ask for your money. It warns you someone else is taking it."*
- **ON-SCREEN:** "YOUR ACCOUNT IS AT RISK"
- **VO:** "The most effective scam call doesn't ask you for money. It warns you that someone else is trying to take it. Your bank's real name shows on the screen — caller ID can be forged. The voice is calm, gives a case number, and knows your recent transactions. Then comes the instruction: for your protection, move your balance into a safe account we've opened for you. And you do it. You transfer it yourself, feeling grateful, because the moment you believe someone is defending you, you stop defending yourself. That's the entire design. Every warm feeling you have during that call is the product. This is now one of the largest categories of fraud loss in the UK."
- **VISUALS:** An incoming call screen showing a bank's name and number → a banking app mid-transfer → a pair of hands, no face → a headset on a bare anonymous desk → a transfer confirmation, then the same balance at zero.
- **END:** *"No real bank will ever ask you to move money to keep it safe. That sentence has no honest version."*
- **TITLE:** The scam call that pretends to protect you
- **DESC:** They don't ask for your money — they warn you it's at risk, then you move it yourself. #scamalert #banking #fraud #scams #shorts
- **SOURCE:** Authorised push payment (APP) fraud; UK Finance annual fraud reporting and Which? consumer investigations. Composite of the standard bank-impersonation pattern.

## 10 — "Why gift cards" · *The payment tell*

- **HOOK (0–2s):** *"No government on Earth accepts gift cards. So why do scammers always ask for them?"*
- **ON-SCREEN:** WHY GIFT CARDS?
- **VO:** "No tax office, no police force, no utility company on Earth accepts payment in gift cards. So why is it always gift cards? Because of what happens to the money. A bank transfer leaves a trail, has a name at the other end, and can sometimes be clawed back. A gift card is different. Once you read the numbers off the back to someone on the phone, the value is gone — instantly, anonymously, and from anywhere in the world. The physical card in your hand is already empty. That's why they never want the card posted. They only ever want the numbers, and they want you to stay on the line while you read them."
- **VISUALS:** A supermarket gift card rack shot straight on → a single card, face down, the scratch strip revealed → a split diagram: bank wire, traceable and reversible in red / gift card, gone in green → a hand holding a card that is visibly, uselessly plastic.
- **END:** *"Anyone who demands gift cards has told you exactly what they are."*
- **TITLE:** Why every scammer asks for gift cards
- **DESC:** The card in your hand is empty the second you read the numbers out. That's the whole reason. #scamalert #giftcards #fraud #scams #shorts
- **SOURCE:** FTC Consumer Protection Data Spotlight on gift cards as a scam payment method; FTC and AARP consumer alerts.

## Production notes

### Voice direction
The failure mode for this niche is sounding entertained. Read it like you're
explaining a case file to someone who's about to be targeted — flat, certain,
unhurried. The material is dramatic enough on its own; if the delivery pushes,
the video tips into mockery and the comment section turns on the victims.

Two paces per script: the "make it normal" beat (the second beat) goes slightly
slower and warmer. The mechanism beat goes slightly faster and colder. That
single contrast does more for retention than any edit.

### Batch production plan
These ten are deliberately ordered so the cheapest ones can carry the launch:

- **Shoot-tonight tier (#4, #10):** pure typography and product photography. No
  scene generation at all. Make these first — they're the format test.
- **Screen tier (#1, #3, #9):** phone UI, message threads, app screens. Build
  one phone-frame template and reuse it across all three.
- **Diagram tier (#5, #6):** one animated explainer diagram each, plus b-roll.
- **Period tier (#7, #8):** the expensive ones. Batch the image generation in a
  single session so the lighting and grade stay consistent between them.

### Reusable assets worth building once
- **One phone frame** (dark, held slightly off-axis, consistent glow) — reused
  across #1, #3, #9 and most of the back catalogue.
- **One "anonymous desk"** — headset, cheap monitor, no face. Appears in #1, #9
  and every phone-scam script you'll ever write.
- **One funnel/flow diagram style** — flat, two colours, thick strokes. Serves
  #4, #5, #6 and the whole Ponzi and dark-patterns series.
- **Two title cards** — modern lane and historical lane, per the palette in the
  idea bank. The viewer should know which series they're in before you speak.

### Description boilerplate
Append to every description, under the hashtags:

> Educational only. This channel explains how scams work so they stop working.
> If you think you've been targeted, report it to your local fraud authority
> and contact your bank directly — using the number on your own card.

### Comment seeding
Pin one question per video. The highest-performing shape in this niche is
"which one of these has been tried on you?" — it invites a story rather than an
opinion, and it keeps the thread sympathetic to victims instead of superior to
them. Never pin anything that invites people to say they'd never fall for it.
